I've picked up "On Photography" by Susan Sontag from the UL recently and have been slowly reading through, slow as in weekly. Every time I manage to sit down though, which I actually need (due to the state of excess dopamine and anxiety I've successfully bored myself into), I felt intentional and calm reading this book and it always leaves me feeling packed with freshness and something new. 

I want to push myself to think deeper, however, as I realise how poor my language and literature skills have been. It may not come naturally nor easily to me, but anything can be practiced and the practice itself is a form of training for resilience and ability to learn. 

Today, I finished the second chapter. When I started last week, I didn't know what to expect with a title indicating a very specific context: America, Seen Through Photographs, Darkly. Initial questions may be: How dark can it be? How can photographs, a very universal thing, be distinct enough to be American? It almost sounded like something to do with politics and culture, rather than photography. Sontag was an American critic, but I wasn't sure if I was interested in American photography specifically. With these doubts in mind, I read on. 

This chapter proved itself to be extremely fruitful. Sontag introduced a few influential figures in photography culture and how it is through these notions that photography grew and became or didn't become what it was for the time. With America as an example, I've realised that photography as a form of media for expression, persuasion, depiction and concealment, is not separate and cannot be separate from political, cultural and societal influences. In fact, it is a produce of all of these factors, our desire as humans to serve a purpose that is meaningful on varying scales. Some interesting points were: photographs as idealised images, inevitability of photographs according value to their subjects, the philosophical question of beauty vs ugliness. 

The majority of the chapter was centred around Diane Arbus. Sontag introduced her as a photographer of the victims and the unfortunate, with no compassion, then went much deeper to show how this had cultural significance in breaking the traditional interpretations of photography. My focus was mainly around 1-2 pages of text that i found particularly intriguing, I wrote down the following immediately after to consolidate my reading: 

-- Arbus used her camera as a weapon in disguise, to access people's lives temporarily, confronting the strange and queasy realities hidden from our daily lives. She challenged the boundary between the morally and emotionally intolerable and what was considered socially acceptable. To photograph the shocking, painful, or embarrassing was to embrace and declare acceptance. By sharing her works with public viewers, Arbus was inviting her audiences to extend their moral threshold by recognising an invisible truth. -- 

I have to be honest, my understanding of the text was probably limited, and possibly incorrect. I found searching up Arbus' photography images helpful as I read the descriptions of her work. I feel like I needed more context and sources to fully uncover the story, which is probably the point of reading! I have questions: What really is a capitalist society - many behaviours stem from this, but how? This is a recurring theme and maybe a dedicated reading on this would help. 

Personally, I am in awe of Arbus' work. She was breaking free from her own somewhat privileged but sheltered background, seeking thrill from the conventionally dangerous and unknowns. I cannot imagine myself doing what she did. Maybe I am not curious enough, maybe I am too content with my current life. But historically there was only one Arbus and her work remained influential for a reason. 

Hope to read on soon.
